# Mathematical background

## Stability theory

Ulam asked whether approximate additive maps must be close to exact additive maps. Hyers proved the sharp constant-one theorem for Banach spaces. Rassias introduced power controls, and later authors developed generalized stability, superstability, and fixed-point methods.

A stability theorem must specify the equation, domain, codomain, approximation condition, exact solution class, and quantitative error. Boundedness, existence, uniqueness, and exactness are distinct properties.

## Quadratic equations

The group form `q(xy)+q(xy⁻¹)=2q(x)+2q(y)` is equivalent to the Jordan–von Neumann equation on abelian groups. On uniquely 2-divisible targets, exact solutions are diagonal values of symmetric biadditive maps. Torsion in the target can create additional exact solutions.

Forti's dyadic construction gives a homogeneous approximant `Q(x²)=4Q(x)` on arbitrary groups. Full quadraticity requires an additional cross-defect limit condition.

## Pexider equations

Pexider equations replace one unknown function by several functions. They have gauge freedoms and, on semigroups without zero, additional invisible values. A sharp theorem must account for these freedoms rather than assuming uniqueness.

## Non-Archimedean phenomena

Ultrametric inequalities prevent the accumulation of errors in finite sums. This yields sharp constants in many additive and Pexider settings. However, when the residue characteristic is two, cancellation in quadratic-type equations can produce arbitrarily small defects without similarly small distance from exact solutions.

## Verification hierarchy

The project uses direct proofs, adversarial review, exact finite computations, floating-point counterexample searches, and Lean 4. Numerical agreement is never treated as proof, and AI-assisted reviews are not represented as external peer review.
